The first paper is titled Follicle-associated epithelial tuft cells amplify type 2 mucosal immunity and IgA production. The lead author is Gabriel M Barrón from the Program in Immunology at Stanford University School of Medicine in Stanford California. Here is what the study found. Peyers patches in the intestinal mucosa coordinate antibody responses to luminal microbes and dietary antigens. The follicle-associated epithelium is traditionally recognized by the presence of microfold cells or M cells that mediate antigen sampling in Peyers patches. By analyzing Gfi1b-eGFP reporter mice and human Peyers patch biopsies the researchers identified tuft cells as an additional evolutionarily conserved follicle-associated epithelium population that regulates mucosal immunity. Using mouse models and intestinal organoids they demonstrate that M cells and tuft cells are distinct lineages arising from Pou2f3 positive progenitors whose differentiation is reciprocally regulated by RANKL and interleukin-13 respectively. Tuft cell-derived IL-25 activates type 2 innate lymphoid cells or ILC2s in Peyers patches. Furthermore IL-13 production by ILC2 enhances immunoglobulin A class switching and antigen-specific immunity. These findings reveal a dual surveillance system in the follicle-associated epithelium where developmentally related epithelial lineages use distinct mechanisms to coordinate mucosal immunity. This is a genuinely exciting discovery because it changes how we think about the gut immune surveillance machinery. We always knew M cells were important players in Peyers patches but now tuft cells are stepping into the spotlight as a key partner in that system, and together they regulate IgA production which is central to mucosal defense. Really elegant work. The second paper from Science Immunology is titled The IKK complex and TBK1 slash IKKε are recruited to STING through distinct mechanisms to activate NF-κB signaling. The first author is Anna Hvarregaard Christensen from the Department of Molecular Biology and Genetics at Aarhus University in Aarhus Denmark. Let me walk you through what they found. Stimulator of interferon genes or STING is a highly conserved signaling molecule that coordinates an antiviral innate immune response through activation of the nuclear factor κB and interferon regulatory factor 3 signaling pathways. In addition to an antiviral response STING-mediated NF-κB signaling also leads to an inflammatory response which can cause substantial pathology. However the molecular mechanism whereby STING drives inflammation is poorly understood and this has hindered the development of anti-inflammatory strategies targeting this pathway. Here the researchers define an evolutionarily conserved mechanism whereby STING activates NF-κB signaling via recruitment of inhibitor of NF-κB kinase subunit γ or IKKγ in an M1-linked ubiquitin-dependent manner. They also show that the more recently evolved carboxyl-terminal tail of STING cooperates with the IKKγ-IKK complex for NF-κB-dependent expression of pro-inflammatory genes in mammalian cells through the kinases TBK1 which stands for TANK-binding kinase 1 and IKKε. Thus the expression of the key inflammatory genes CCL3 and CCL4 requires the formation of M1-linked ubiquitin and the activation of NF-κB but the researchers do not detect a role for IRF3 in STING-driven NF-κB signaling. So what makes this important is that STING has been a hot target in the field for a while now because it sits at this critical junction between sensing danger signals in the cell and triggering immune responses. But the inflammatory side of STING signaling has been kind of a black box mechanistically and this paper starts to crack that open in a really meaningful way. The third Science Immunology paper published October 9th is titled B1-like innate B cells are present in adult human organs in homeostasis. The first author is Ondrej Suchanek from the Molecular Immunity Unit at the University of Cambridge Department of Medicine in Cambridge UK. Here is the abstract. Innate-like B cells produce natural antibodies and include marginal zone B cells and B1 cells. B1 cells are well characterized in mice where they prenatally seed serous cavities and produce regulatory cytokines. Putative B1 cells have been identified in human prenatal organs but whether they persist into adulthood remains unclear. Here the researchers identified B1-like cells in the IgM positive and IgA positive memory compartment across adult human organs. These cells transcriptionally resembled murine and human prenatal B1 cells but not marginal zone B cells. They expressed canonical murine B1 cell markers including AHNAK and spontaneously secreted IgM. B cell receptor sequencing of paired kidney-spleen samples revealed lower diversity and mutation rates in kidney IgM and IgA clones suggesting spleen-independent seeding. Adult B1-like cells expressed the regulatory cytokine TGFB and had abundant inferred interactions with tissue myeloid cells which reciprocally expressed B cell prosurvival cytokines. These findings support the presence of B1-like cells across adult human organs in homeostasis. This one is fascinating because B1 cells have been kind of considered a mouse thing for a long time. The idea that they exist as a persistent population in adult humans across multiple organs opens up a whole new chapter in our understanding of innate B cell biology in humans.
