The paper is titled Gut microbial functional associations with subsequent irritable bowel syndrome in a prospective paediatric case-control study. It was published on October 30th in EBioMedicine, and the first author is Bryan Cordova from the Department of Microbiology and Cell Science at the University of Florida in Gainesville. So here is what this study is all about. We know that the infant gut microbiome, meaning the community of microorganisms living in a baby's intestines, goes through rapid and dramatic changes in the first year or two of life. These changes are thought to have lasting effects on gastrointestinal health throughout a person's life. Adults who have irritable bowel syndrome, or IBS, which is a condition causing chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and changes in bowel habits, tend to show characteristic differences in their microbial communities and their metabolism. But here is the key question the researchers asked: do these differences actually come before the disease starts, or do they develop as a consequence of it? To answer this, the team used stool samples collected from a large cohort called the All Babies in Southeast Sweden cohort, or ABIS, when the babies were around twelve and a half months old on average. They then followed these children forward in time using what is called a nested case-control design, comparing children who went on to develop IBS or pelvic and abdominal pain with those who did not. The average gap between when the stool was collected and when a diagnosis was made was almost twelve years, and the average age at diagnosis was around twelve and a half years. So we are talking about looking at baby poop and predicting gut disease in preteens. That is remarkable. The researchers analyzed the microbial composition using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which is a technique that identifies bacteria by reading a specific genetic marker. They processed the data using a pipeline called DADA2, assigned the bacteria using a reference database called SILVA, and then predicted what functions those microbial communities might be carrying out using a computational tool called PICRUSt2. They used several statistical approaches to identify differences, including PERMANOVA for global differences, ANCOM-BC2 for differential abundance, and GSEA for pathway enrichment. What they found is striking. Infants who would later go on to develop IBS showed a microbial profile that diverged from healthy controls in several important ways. Specifically, they had lower levels of short-chain fatty acid producers, which are bacteria that ferment dietary fiber and produce beneficial compounds that nourish the gut lining and regulate immunity. At the same time, these at-risk infants had higher levels of what the researchers called facultative and mucosa-disruptive taxa, meaning bacteria that can damage the protective mucosal layer of the intestine. When they looked at the predicted functional profiles, the at-risk infants showed reduced fermentative capacity, meaning their gut bugs were less capable of breaking down complex carbohydrates through fermentation. They also showed greater respiratory potential, accelerated degradation of neuroactive aromatic amines, which are molecules that can affect the nervous system and gut signaling, and enrichment of something called the cytochrome bc1 complex respiratory unit. Putting this all together, the researchers conclude that gut microbiome dysbiosis in infants who are at risk for IBS is present many years before the diagnosis is made. The early disruptions in fermentation, redox balance, and amine metabolism may compromise what they call epithelial resilience, meaning the gut lining's ability to maintain its protective barrier, and this could promote visceral hypersensitivity, which is the heightened sensitivity to gut sensations that is a hallmark of IBS. Excitingly, the authors suggest that this early detection of microbiome dysbiosis opens the door to probiotic-based interventions long before symptoms ever appear. Now let us move into some truly cutting-edge immunology, and I have three papers from Science Immunology for you today. These are fantastic and they are all connected in an interesting way. The first is a brief but important perspective piece titled Metabolic licensing of innate immune memory, published October 30th in Science Immunology. The first author is Musa M Mhlanga from the Department of Cell Biology at the Radboud Institute for Molecular Life Sciences at Radboud University in Nijmegen in the Netherlands. This piece ties together a concept that is getting a lot of attention in immunology right now, which is trained immunity. Trained immunity is the idea that innate immune cells, the first responders of your immune system, can actually develop a kind of memory after being exposed to a stimulus, even though traditionally we thought only adaptive immune cells like T and B cells could do that. The abstract of this perspective reveals that NAD+, which stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and is a molecule central to cellular energy metabolism, turns out to be a metabolic signal that preserves an epigenetic mark central to trained immunity. It does this by inhibiting a histone demethylase called KDM5A. If some of those terms sound complex, do not worry, because the next paper goes into all of this in wonderful detail. That next paper is titled Nuclear NAD+ promotes innate immune memory by inhibiting KDM5A-dependent histone demethylation, also published October 30th in Science Immunology. The first author is Huan Jin from the State Key Laboratory of Oncology in South China, at the Guangdong Provincial Clinical Research Center for Cancer, at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center in Guangzhou, China. This is the research article that the perspective piece was commenting on, and it is a really exciting piece of science. So let me walk you through it. Trained immunity, as I mentioned, is tightly regulated by two intertwined processes: metabolic reprogramming, meaning changes in how cells generate and use energy, and epigenetic reprogramming, meaning changes in how genes are turned on or off without altering the underlying DNA sequence. But how these two processes talk to each other at the level of the genome to establish long-term innate immune memory has not been fully understood. Until now. What Huan Jin and colleagues showed is this: when trained immunity is induced, the nucleus of the cell accumulates higher levels of NAD+. This nuclear NAD+ then directly binds to KDM5A, which is a histone demethylase, an enzyme that removes methyl groups from histones, which are the proteins that DNA wraps around. By binding to and inhibiting KDM5A, NAD+ prevents it from removing a specific methyl mark called H3K4me3, which stands for histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation. This mark sits at the promoters of inflammatory genes and keeps chromatin, which is the packaged form of DNA, in an accessible and open state. That accessibility is what allows those genes to be rapidly switched on again when the immune cell encounters a threat for the second time, which is the essence of immune memory. The team then tested what happens when you disrupt NAD+ production. They genetically deleted or pharmacologically blocked an enzyme called NAMPT, which stands for nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase, and that is the rate-limiting enzyme for NAD+ synthesis. When NAMPT was knocked out, trained immunity was impaired, and so was associated antitumor activity. But here is the really hopeful translational piece: supplementing with NMN, or nicotinamide mononucleotide, a precursor to NAD+, actually rescued the defective trained immunity and antitumor activity in NAMPT-deficient and aged mice. And in human cancer, they found that NAMPT expression in myeloid cells, which are a type of innate immune cell, correlated with a more inflammatory tumor microenvironment. The conclusion is clear: KDM5A acts as a nuclear NAD+ sensor that orchestrates the histone methylation and chromatin accessibility required for trained immunity. It is titled Antibodies to distinct epitopes on MPXV enveloped virion provide additive and complete protection, published October 30th in Science Immunology. The first author is Ding Zhang from the Department of Integrative Structural and Computational Biology at the Scripps Research Institute in La Jolla, California. Monkeypox virus, or MPXV, has been in the news in recent years, and for good reason. Its continuing evolution into clades capable of sustained human-to-human transmission poses an escalating global health risk. Yet we currently have no effective therapy specifically for poxvirus infection. This study set out to investigate a panel of human monoclonal antibodies, which are precisely engineered proteins that recognize and bind to specific targets on the virus. The antibodies studied here target two antigens, called A35 and B6, both located on something called the extracellular enveloped virion, or EV. The EV is one of the two main forms of the virus, and it is particularly important for spread between cells and between people. Four of the high-affinity antibodies target what the researchers describe as the central groove of the A35 dimer, a structural feature of the A35 protein. One antibody targets a brand new, previously unidentified epitope on what they call the A35 protruding loop. Importantly, all of these epitopes are conserved across most orthopoxviruses, meaning they are shared structural features found in related viruses like vaccinia and variola. The key finding is this: when researchers combined one anti-A35 antibody with one anti-B6 antibody, they saw additive and complete protection from both morbidity, meaning disease, and mortality in a lethal vaccinia virus mouse model. This challenges a long-standing assumption in the field that you need antibodies targeting both the EV form and the other main form of the virus called the mature virion, or MV, to achieve full protection. The findings open up new possibilities for next-generation therapeutics and vaccines against emerging MPXV and other orthopoxvirus threats. This study, published October 30th in Science Advances, is titled Super-resolution mapping of nuclear stress signaling by probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles in UV-induced skin photodamage. The first author is Ga-Eun Go from the Department of Chemistry at Hanyang University in Seoul, South Korea. Ultraviolet radiation, or UV, from the sun drives oxidative stress, DNA damage, and chronic inflammation in skin cells, leading to progressive cellular damage and loss of skin function, which we call photodamage. Probiotics have been gaining attention as potential anti-photodamage agents, but exactly how they work at the nanoscale level has not been explored until now. This team used super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, an incredibly detailed imaging technique, to investigate how extracellular vesicles derived from a probiotic bacterium called Lactobacillus plantarum protect against photodamage. Extracellular vesicles are tiny membrane-enclosed particles released by bacteria that can carry bioactive molecules and influence cells they encounter. The researchers found that UV irradiation triggered extensive remodeling of cellular organelles, along with elevated reactive oxygen species production, DNA damage signaling, and inflammatory activation. When they treated cells with the Lactobacillus plantarum extracellular vesicles either before or after UV exposure, the organelle integrity was preserved and the native nanoscale architecture of the cells was restored. Furthermore, the extracellular vesicle treatment delayed UV-induced PARP-1 activation, which is a key player in the DNA damage response, and it delayed nuclear condensate clustering. It also suppressed the translocation of NF-κB, which is a master transcription factor for inflammation, into the nucleus, and reduced downstream inflammatory cytokine expression. The authors conclude that probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles are modulators of stress-responsive signaling and structural homeostasis during UV-induced photodamage. Pyroptosis is particularly exciting in this context because it releases danger signals that can kick-start an immune response against the tumor. And at the same time, the platform alleviates metabolic constraints on activated T cells, preserving CTL viability and their ability to sustain effector and memory functions. In a murine colon cancer model, low-dose radiotherapy combined with this platform achieved more than ninety-five percent tumor growth inhibition. When combined further with anti-PD-L1 therapy, which is a type of cancer immunotherapy, it induced regression of bilateral triple-negative breast tumors and prevented lung metastasis.
