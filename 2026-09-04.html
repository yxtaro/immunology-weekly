We are going to start with a study that asks a surprisingly fundamental question about our organs. Why do some tissues have more macrophages than others? The paper is titled Tissue mitochondrial activity dictates the macrophage pool size and it was published on September 4th 2026 in Science Immunology. The first author is Laura Pena-Couso from the Centro Nacional de Investigaciones Cardiovasculares, known as CNIC Carlos III, in Madrid Spain. So here is the setup. Every tissue in our body produces cellular waste and that waste needs to be cleared out. Macrophages are the cleanup crew. But it has never been entirely clear how tissues match the number of macrophages they have to the amount of waste they produce. This study goes looking for that mechanism and what they found is really elegant. The authors show that parenchymal cells in our organs are constantly releasing mitochondria into the extracellular space and macrophages are picking those up. And this is especially prominent in organs that rely heavily on oxidative respiration, meaning organs that run on high metabolic activity like the heart, skeletal muscle, and brown adipose tissue. What is more, the number of macrophages in a tissue very closely matches how metabolically active that tissue is, how much mitochondrial activity is going on. The way this works mechanistically is that the mitochondrial activity of myofibers, those are the muscle cells, actually controls how much of a signaling molecule called colony-stimulating factor 1 or CSF1 is available in the tissue. It does this by regulating how many CSF1-producing fibroblasts are present. CSF1 is a key survival and proliferation signal for macrophages. So more mitochondrial activity means more CSF1 means more macrophages. And when the researchers blocked CSF1 signaling through its receptor called CSF1R, the macrophages disappeared and the skeletal muscle lost its mitochondrial activity. So the relationship goes both ways. The macrophages and the mitochondrial activity of the tissue are mutually dependent on each other. The authors propose that this coupling is how tissues ensure efficient waste disposal and stay fit. It is a beautiful feedback loop hiding in plain sight. Next up we have another study from Science Immunology dated September 4th 2026. This one is titled Positive selection of IgG over IgM plasma cells through BCR isotype-specific antigen presentation and signaling and the first author is Yuki Tai from the Laboratory of Lymphocyte Differentiation at the WPI Immunology Frontier Research Center at the University of Osaka in Japan. This paper is about a puzzle in B cell biology. When your immune system mounts a primary response to an infection, B cells start out expressing an immunoglobulin M B cell receptor, which is called IgM BCR. Some of those B cells then switch over to expressing an IgG BCR through a process called isotype switching. Now we have known for a while that B cells that have switched to IgG tend to give rise to more long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow compared to those still expressing IgM. But the question has been why. What is it about the IgG isotype that gives those cells a survival and differentiation advantage? Yuki Tai and colleagues figured out at least two important parts of the answer. First they found that IgG1-expressing germinal center B cells are better at presenting antigen to T follicular helper cells compared to IgM-expressing B cells. And because they present more antigen, they get more help from those T follicular helper cells, which drives greater proliferation of IgG1 plasma cells. Second, when signaling goes through the IgM BCR it triggers more apoptosis through a molecule called Bim, meaning IgM-expressing plasma cells are more likely to die. Put those two things together and you get a situation where IgG1 plasma cells outcompete IgM plasma cells in secondary lymphoid tissues. And on top of that IgG1 plasma cells are more prone to migrating to the bone marrow where they can become long-lived. This research helps us understand why our immune memory is dominated by IgG and has implications for how we think about vaccine design and long-term protection. Still in Science Immunology from September 4th 2026 we have a brief but important perspective piece titled Old dog with new tricks: SARS-CoV-2 variant-specific B cells complement imprinted memory. The first author is Lauren B Rodda from the Department of Molecular Microbiology and Immunology at Oregon Health and Science University in Portland Oregon. The key message here is that when we encounter a new SARS-CoV-2 variant, both existing memory B cell clones and completely newly generated antibody clones contribute to neutralization, but they have distinct origins and they target different parts of the virus. This is a nuanced and important point for understanding how imprinted immune memory works and why our immune systems can still respond to evolving pathogens even when those pathogens look quite different from what originally trained our immune cells. Now here is something that might change how you think about neutrophils, those frontline white blood cells. The paper is titled Adaptive motility enables neutrophils to rapidly navigate confined capillaries and it comes from Science Immunology, also published September 4th 2026. The first author is Mathieu Deygas from Institut Curie and Institut Pierre-Gilles de Gennes at PSL Research University, CNRS UMR 144 in Paris France. Neutrophils need to get to inflamed tissues and to do that they have to physically squeeze through capillaries that can be as narrow as two micrometers. That is incredibly tight. And the question the authors are asking is how do they do this without blocking blood flow or damaging the capillary itself? Using a really clever combination of intravital live-cell imaging, meaning watching this happen in living animals, combined with biomimetic microdevices that mimic capillary dimensions in the lab, the researchers found something remarkable. Neutrophils actually maintain their migration speed even as capillaries get progressively narrower. This is not something other leukocytes can do. And interestingly it does not have anything to do with the properties of the nucleus, which is often thought to be the main physical barrier to squeezing through tight spaces. Instead what happens is that confinement triggers a rapid engagement of something called Rho-dependent actomyosin contractility at the rear of the cell. Essentially the back of the neutrophil contracts to push it through. The tighter the space, the more contractility kicks in to compensate. When the researchers disrupted this adaptive response, neutrophils got stuck, causing jamming and eventually blocking the capillary networks entirely. So this is a neutrophil-intrinsic mechanical sensing and adaptation system that helps keep blood vessels open and functional even during inflammation. Really elegant work. We also have another short piece from Science Immunology this week titled The many roads to B cell memory. The first author is Oluwagbemiga A Ojo from the Department of Medicine Division of Allergy and Immunology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago Illinois. The finding highlighted here is that germinal center-experienced IgM memory B cells require BCL6 to form but do not actually need T follicular helper cells for their generation. Let us move now to a very exciting translational piece from Science Advances published September 4th 2026 titled Supramolecular immunomodulatory hydrogelator potentiates CAR-T therapy with long-lasting endogenous immunity toward solid tumor eradication. The first author is Chenwei Jiang from the School of Biomedical Engineering at Shanghai Jiao Tong University in Shanghai China. CAR-T cell therapy has been transformative for blood cancers but it has struggled with solid tumors. The reasons for this are several. CAR-T cells have trouble infiltrating solid tumors, the tumor microenvironment is immunosuppressive and tends to exhaust those T cells, and the therapy often does not produce lasting immunity. This study addresses all of these challenges in one system. The researchers developed an immunomodulatory drug-drug conjugate-based hydrogelator that can be injected directly into a tumor. Once inside it forms a gel scaffold that acts as a sustained-release reservoir. And what it releases over time is a combination of CAR-T cells plus two immunomodulatory agents. The first is NLG919 which is an inhibitor of an enzyme called IDO-1 that tumors use to suppress immune responses. The second is DPPA-1 which is a peptide that blocks PD-L1, a checkpoint molecule that tumors use to shut down T cells. Together these agents remodel the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment from the inside out. The results in mouse models were impressive. In aggressive melanoma, metastatic breast cancer, and postoperative glioma models, a single local administration of this hydrogel significantly suppressed tumor growth and also prevented tumor recurrence and metastasis upon rechallenge. The system also stimulated a durable endogenous immune response and established long-lasting immunological memory. This is a platform that could make CAR-T therapy clinically viable for a much wider range of cancers. From Science Advances also published September 4th 2026 we have a fascinating study combining chemistry and immunology. Still in Science Advances September 4th 2026 we have a clinical story. The paper is titled Synthetic long peptide and DNA personalized cancer vaccines induce robust neoantigen-specific T cell responses in pancreatic cancer. The first author is Felicia Zhang Perkins from the Department of Surgery at Washington University School of Medicine in Saint Louis Missouri. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, or PDAC, is one of the most treatment-resistant cancers we know of. It does not respond well to standard immunotherapies even though it does harbor cancer neoantigens that in principle could trigger T cell responses. This study reports on two phase 1 clinical trials that evaluated personalized cancer vaccines made from either synthetic long peptides or DNA constructs targeting patient-specific neoantigens. Still for a cancer as lethal as pancreatic cancer, even a trend is meaningful and this work validates the feasibility of neoantigen-targeted personalized vaccines. Next from Science Advances September 4th 2026 is a paper titled Cardiomyocyte-intrinsic somatic mtDNA mutations induce an OXPHOS-dependent immune response and promote progressive heart failure. The first author is Kristina Bubb from the Department of Medical Biochemistry and Biophysics at Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm Sweden. Mitochondrial DNA mutations accumulate as we age but their specific contribution to aging has been hard to pin down because animal models used to study this affect all tissues simultaneously, making it hard to know if what you see is a local effect or a systemic one. This study took a more precise approach by generating mice called PolgiMut mice where mitochondrial DNA mutations can be induced specifically in cardiomyocytes and in a controlled time window. When mutations were induced in heart cells these mice developed progressive contractile dysfunction and respiratory chain deficiency in the heart but without systemic disease. Proteomic analyses revealed something very interesting: the cardiac dysfunction was linked to a progressive immune response characterized by upregulation of antigen-processing proteins and immune cell infiltration into the heart. Meanwhile longevity-associated pathways were suppressed and appeared to be operating independently of the mitochondrial and immune changes. This work establishes a direct mechanistic link between cardiomyocyte-intrinsic mitochondrial DNA mutations, immune activation, and heart failure, which is directly relevant to understanding age-related cardiac disease. Now from Science Advances September 4th 2026 we have a very exciting RNA therapeutics paper. It is titled Messenger RNA delivery of a dual CD25/CD122 affinity-tuned IL-2 variant prolongs exposure and potentiates antitumor T cell immunity. The first author is Mathias Vormehr from BioNTech SE in Mainz Germany. Interleukin-2 or IL-2 is a powerful immune stimulant with known antitumor activity but its clinical use has been limited by serious toxicity and the fact that it activates regulatory T cells which suppress immune responses alongside the CD8+ effector T cells you actually want to activate. This study describes a really sophisticated engineering solution. The researchers designed an IL-2 variant with reduced binding to IL-2 receptor alpha, also called CD25, which is preferentially expressed on regulatory T cells, and enhanced binding to IL-2 receptor beta, also called CD122, which is preferentially expressed on CD8+ effector T cells. This dual tuning selectively stimulates the effector cells while sparing regulatory T cells. They then fused this variant to albumin for better pharmacokinetics and packaged it as messenger RNA in a lipid nanoparticle so that when you give it intravenously the liver produces the protein in a sustained way over time. They call this construct Alb-IL-2var RNA-LNP. In both mice and cynomolgus monkeys the construct had a favorable pharmacokinetic profile and was tolerated at biologically active doses. It selectively expanded CD8+ T cells over regulatory T cells in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells in vitro and in animal models in vivo. When combined with an mRNA cancer vaccine in syngeneic mouse tumor models it drove expansion of tumor-infiltrating and circulating tumor antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. In advanced and immunologically cold tumors it enhanced the efficacy of radiotherapy, checkpoint inhibitors, and cancer vaccines, and combination with checkpoint inhibitors and vaccine induced what the authors describe as a profound proinflammatory conversion of cold tumors. This is a compelling preclinical package supporting clinical evaluation for solid cancers. Let us move to the Journal of Experimental Medicine with a paper published October 5th 2026 titled Human-inherited RORγT deficiency encompasses genetic heterogeneity, T cell deficiency, and clinical homogeneity. The first author is Iris Fagniez from the St. Giles Laboratory of Human Genetics of Infectious Diseases, Rockefeller Branch, at The Rockefeller University in New York. RORγT is a transcription factor critical for the development of certain T cell populations. Previous work from this group had described RORγT deficiency in seven patients across three ancestries who had infections with mycobacteria and chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, a persistent fungal infection of the skin and mucous membranes. This paper adds five more patients from entirely different ancestries, Afghan, Indian, Iranian, Japanese, and Sri Lankan, each carrying a new homozygous loss-of-function variant in the RORC gene that encodes RORγT. What makes this paper so informative is that it deeply characterizes the immune defects. All patients showed near-complete depletion of innate-like adaptive T cells including mucosa-associated invariant T cells and invariant natural killer T cells. They also had low counts of adaptive TH1 star and CD8+ T cells and impaired interferon gamma production in response to mycobacteria from multiple immune cell types including NK cells, conventional CD4+ T cells, and both Vdelta 1 and Vdelta 2 gamma delta T cells. Most patients also had low TH17 and TH1 star cell counts explaining the susceptibility to candida. One patient died from disseminated Bacille Calmette-Guerin vaccine infection but remarkably all other patients are alive and clinically stable despite these profound immune deficiencies. This study firmly establishes RORγT as essential for protective immunity against both mycobacteria and candida in humans and expands our picture of the global genetic diversity of this condition. From Nature Microbiology published September 3rd 2026 we have a paper titled IL-17A-producing γδ T cells control pulmonary Mycobacterium abscessus infection in mice. The first author is Xiaoqian Hu from the Department of Infectious Diseases of Huashan Hospital and the Department of Immunology at the School of Basic Medical Sciences, Shanghai Medical College, Fudan University in Shanghai China. Mycobacterium abscessus is a nontuberculous mycobacterium that causes serious lung infections especially in people with underlying lung disease or weakened immunity. The role of gamma delta T cells in these infections has not been well understood. This study set out to characterize the immune landscape of the lungs in a mouse model of M. abscessus infection. The key finding is that IL-17A-producing gamma delta T cells are essential for controlling the infection. These cells actually directly eliminate extracellular bacteria. They were protective in two distinct mouse models, one with pulmonary fibrosis and one lacking functional interferon gamma mediated immunity, which is important because many patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial infections have compromised interferon gamma responses. Mechanistically these cells kill bacteria through granzyme B-dependent cytotoxic pathways and the IL-17A they produce is not just a bystander. It is cell-intrinsically required for the gamma delta T cells own activation and bacterial recognition. Without IL-17A those cells lose their ability to recognize and kill the bacteria. The researchers also worked out the upstream signals. M. abscessus is recognized by Toll-like receptor 2 on macrophages, which then produce IL-1 beta and IL-23, and those cytokines drive the expansion and activation of the IL-17A-producing gamma delta T cells. Finally single-cell RNA sequencing on human peripheral blood mononuclear cells suggested that anti-interferon gamma autoantibodies, which some patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial infections have, may compromise gamma delta T cell function. This work positions IL-17A-producing gamma delta T cells as a promising therapeutic target for nontuberculous mycobacterial infections. Also from Nature Microbiology September 3rd 2026 is a study directly relevant to HIV cure research. The paper is titled BCL-2 inhibition at antiretroviral therapy initiation reduces the intact SIV reservoir in macaques and the first author is Tomas Raul Wiche Salinas from the Division of Microbiology and Immunology at the Emory National Biomedical Research Center at Emory University in Atlanta Georgia. One of the central challenges in curing HIV is eliminating the viral reservoir, the pool of long-lived infected CD4+ T cells that persist even when patients are on antiretroviral therapy. The anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 helps these reservoir cells survive. BCL-2 can be inhibited by a drug called venetoclax which is already approved for certain blood cancers. This study tested whether combining venetoclax with antiretroviral therapy early in infection could reduce the intact SIV reservoir in macaques. The researchers infected 24 rhesus macaques with simian immunodeficiency virus and started antiretroviral therapy 14 days post infection, either alone or combined with a 10-day course of venetoclax, with or without depletion of CD8 alpha cells. They then followed these animals for nearly 300 days. The result was a rapid and sustained reduction of the intact SIV reservoir in blood and lymph nodes in venetoclax-treated animals. Interestingly the CD4+ T cells that survived venetoclax treatment showed signs of partial resistance to apoptosis. They expressed elevated levels of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 and BCL-xL and reduced levels of pro-apoptotic molecules like PUMA. This suggests that the most resistant reservoir cells survive by upregulating alternative survival signals. The authors argue this supports the rationale for longer dosing of venetoclax and combining it with agents that target additional anti-apoptotic molecules to get at the most treatment-resistant reservoir cells. This is meaningful progress toward an HIV cure strategy. Now from the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences published September 15th 2026 we have a conceptually rich paper titled One mechanism, two diseases: Involvement of enzyme-substrate complexes in the pathogenesis of celiac disease and rheumatoid arthritis. The first author is Ludvig M Sollid from the KG Jebsen Center for Specific Autoimmune Therapy and Norwegian Coeliac Disease Research Centre at the Institute of Clinical Medicine, University of Oslo in Oslo Norway. This is a perspective piece that draws a compelling parallel between two very different autoimmune diseases. In celiac disease HLA class II allotypes are the main genetic risk factor, patients have antibodies to deamidated peptides from gluten, and they have autoantibodies to transglutaminase 2, the enzyme that deamidates those peptides. Recent work has established that autoreactive B cells internalize complexes of transglutaminase 2 bound to its substrate, process those complexes, and present the modified peptides to CD4+ T cells. This enzyme-substrate complex mechanism is the mechanistic engine driving the disease. Sollid argues that rheumatoid arthritis follows the same logic. There too HLA class II allotypes dominate the genetics, there are antibodies to citrullinated peptides, and there are autoantibodies to the enzymes that produce citrullination, namely peptidyl arginine deiminase 2 and 4. By analogy, autoreactive B cells in rheumatoid arthritis may be internalizing enzyme-substrate complexes and presenting citrullinated peptides to CD4+ T cells. This would explain why B cell depleting therapies work in rheumatoid arthritis. And it raises a really provocative question: if celiac disease is driven by exogenous gluten antigens activating this pathway, might rheumatoid arthritis also be driven by a foreign antigen that has not yet been identified? It is a unifying framework that could reshape how we think about these conditions. From PNAS September 8th 2026 we have an exciting mRNA therapy paper titled Intratumoral injection of EpCAM BITE, IL-12, and GM-CSF mRNA-LNPs blocks the growth of local treated and distant untreated tumors. The first author is Vita Golubovskaya from Promab Biotechnologies in Richmond California. This study builds on previous work showing that a bispecific antibody that simultaneously engages EpCAM on tumor cells and CD3 on T cells could be delivered as mRNA inside lipid nanoparticles directly into a tumor. The new question was whether adding cytokines to that local injection could extend the immune response to tumors on the other side of the body, a phenomenon known as the abscopal effect. The researchers tested this in mice with tumors on both the left and right sides, injecting only into the left side. Combining IL-12 and GM-CSF with the anti-EpCAM-CD3 bispecific antibody mRNA significantly increased T cell numbers and activated T cell killing pathways in both the injected and the uninjected tumor. It also substantially upregulated HLA class II expression in both tumors, essentially converting cancer cells into potential antigen-presenting cells. This systemic effect was achieved through local delivery, which matters because systemic delivery of IL-12 in particular has serious toxicity concerns. The authors suggest this platform could provide a broadly applicable approach to immunotherapy across many cancer types and stages. Now from Cell Reports September 3rd 2026 we have a paper titled ACLY palmitoylation reprograms macrophages histone acetylation rewiring to repress hepatocellular carcinoma. The first author is Junlong Zhao from the State Key Laboratory of Holistic Integrative Management of Gastrointestinal Cancers at the Fourth Military Medical University in Xian China. The tumor microenvironment suppresses macrophages in part by reducing their chromatin accessibility, making it harder for them to express pro-inflammatory genes. This study asks how fatty acid metabolism in macrophages connects to their epigenetic state. The researchers found that saturated fatty acids, especially palmitic acid, are lower in tumor-associated macrophages of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. When they knocked out an enzyme called Scd1, which converts saturated to unsaturated fatty acids, palmitic acid accumulated and both primary and metastatic liver cancer progression slowed while overall survival improved. Mechanistically the palmitic acid causes palmitoylation of an enzyme called ATP-citrate lyase or ACLY at a specific residue called C893. This palmitoylation stabilizes ACLY in its active tetrameric form by protecting it from degradation by the CUL3 pathway. A stable active ACLY converts more citrate to acetyl-CoA which is the substrate for histone acetylation. More histone acetylation at M1-related gene loci means macrophages express more pro-inflammatory genes and become better at fighting the tumor. The researchers then validated therapeutic approaches based on this mechanism, including dietary palmitic acid combined with macrophage infusion and in vivo reprogramming strategies, both of which improved the tumor microenvironment and suppressed hepatocellular carcinoma progression. This is a lovely example of how metabolic programming connects to epigenetic and immune outcomes in cancer. From Cell Reports also September 3rd 2026 we have a paper titled The regulation of COX-2, ABCA1, and ABCG1 by the lncRNA PACERR links the inflammatory response and cholesterol homeostasis. The first author is Elizabeth J Hennessy from the Institute for Translational Medicine and Therapeutics at the Perelman School of Medicine, University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia. Cyclooxygenase-2 or COX-2 is a central enzyme in inflammation and its inhibition by drugs like ibuprofen or aspirin is well known. But COX-2 inhibitors carry cardiovascular risk, and one reason may be that COX-2 is connected to cholesterol handling in blood vessels. This paper investigates a long noncoding RNA called PACERR, which stands for PTGS2 antisense complex-mediated expression regulator RNA, and its role in linking these two systems. PACERR expression is induced by both high-density lipoprotein and lipopolysaccharide and it regulates COX-2 as well as two cholesterol transporters called ABCA1 and ABCG1 in vascular smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and macrophages. PACERR physically interacts with regulatory proteins including p300, hnRNPL, and IkappaB-zeta and controls how those proteins associate with target gene regions. When PACERR is silenced, cholesterol efflux induced by high-density lipoprotein increases. COX-2 expression and prostaglandin production change. And the lncRNA also regulates a phospholipase called cPLA2 through nearby regulatory elements, influencing arachidonic acid release. In inflammatory settings silencing PACERR reduces COX-2, lowers prostaglandin biosynthesis, and dampens systemic inflammatory responses. This lncRNA sits at the intersection of inflammation and cholesterol metabolism and represents a potential target for reducing cardiovascular inflammation without the risks of directly inhibiting COX-2.
