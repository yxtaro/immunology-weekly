Let us just dive right in. We are starting with a paper published September 18th 2026 in the journal Cell. The title is mRNA-encoding CD19-targeting T cell engager for refractory immune thrombocytopenia. The first author is Mengyan Wang from the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in Shanghai, China. So here is what this study is all about. You may have heard of CAR-T cell therapy or T cell engagers, which are two exciting approaches being used to deplete B cells in patients with certain autoimmune diseases and cancers. Both of these strategies show real clinical promise, but there are still issues with safety, how well they work, and how long the effects actually last. This research introduces something called ABO2203, which is a lipid nanoparticle-formulated messenger RNA that encodes a CD19-targeting T cell engager. Think of it this way: instead of giving the patient the actual protein drug directly, you are delivering a little genetic instruction in a lipid nanoparticle that tells the body to produce the T cell engager itself. In transgenic mice, ABO2203 caused complete B cell depletion, and importantly, it produced a more attenuated cytokine release compared with just giving the T cell engager as a protein. Cytokine release is something we really want to minimize because too much of it can cause what is called cytokine release syndrome, which can be dangerous. Now, in a first-in-human study involving three patients with refractory secondary immune thrombocytopenia, which is a condition where the immune system attacks platelets, ABO2203 achieved rapid and complete peripheral B cell depletion, with sustained depletion continuing in the bone marrow. The patients showed durable platelet recovery, improved serology, and reduced disease activity over six months of follow-up. And here is the reassuring part: ABO2203 was well tolerated. There were only grade 1 and grade 2 adverse events, and crucially, there was no cytokine release syndrome. After treatment, B cell reconstitution was observed with predominantly transitional and naive B cells coming back. The researchers conclude that mRNA-encoded T cell engagers represent a potent and safer therapeutic modality for B cell-mediated autoimmune diseases. This is a really exciting step forward in using mRNA technology beyond vaccines. Next up, we move to a paper published September 18th 2026 in Cell Metabolism. The title is Glutamine metabolism as a rheostat of CD4 positive T cell pathogenic function in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. The first author is Keisuke Sawada from the Division of Immunobiology at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center, and the Immunology Graduate Program and Medical Scientist Training Program at the University of Cincinnati College of Medicine. So metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, or MASLD for short, is basically a spectrum of liver conditions linked to metabolic problems like obesity and insulin resistance. Its severity has independently been linked to pathogenic CD4 positive T cell responses and something called skewed hepatic glutamine metabolism. Glutamine is an amino acid that plays a major role in immune cell function. But whether these two things, the T cell responses and the glutamine metabolism changes, actually interact to drive disease progression was not really clear. This study identifies hepatic glutamine depletion as a key feature of steatohepatitis. That depletion was linked with increased hepatic CD4 positive T cell inflammation and hepatocellular damage in humans. In mouse models, both total hepatic and hepatic CD4 positive T cell glutamine levels were similarly reduced. When the researchers restored hepatic glutamine through supplementation, it selectively restrained hepatic CD4 positive T cell inflammatory programs and alleviated hepatocellular damage and disease severity. Mechanistically, they found that T cell-intrinsic Gls1-mediated glutaminolysis, which is the process by which glutamine is broken down inside T cells, limited something called O-GlcNAcylation. This limitation in O-GlcNAcylation dampened pathogenic CD4 positive T cell inflammation. Importantly, glutamine treatment also dampened CD4 positive T cell-mediated injury in human liver organoids. Together these findings establish hepatic CD4 positive T cell-intrinsic glutamine metabolism as a critical rheostat of pathogenic inflammation in MASLD and point to metabolism-targeted strategies as a way to slow down disease progression. The idea that something as fundamental as an amino acid can act as a dial controlling immune-driven liver damage is really striking. Now let us talk about a study out of Science Advances, also dated September 18th 2026. The title is Functional diversity of natural human IFNB1 variants in innate immunity. The first author is Samira Schiefer from the Institute of Medical Virology at the University of Zurich in Switzerland. Interferon beta, which is encoded by the IFNB1 gene, is a type I interferon that is absolutely critical for our innate immune defenses against viruses. It is also an approved therapeutic used in conditions like multiple sclerosis. We know that genetic variations in different components of the type I interferon system are associated with infectious and immune diseases, but what happens when IFNB1 itself varies? That was largely unexplored until now. This group functionally evaluated 70 naturally occurring rare protein-altering IFNB1 variants detected in humans globally. What they found was striking. Twenty-five percent of all protein variants were substantially less active than the common reference interferon beta. For many of those less active variants, this correlated with the protein being retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and not being secreted properly, or with a compromised interaction with IFNAR1 and IFNAR2, which are the cell surface receptors that interferon beta needs to bind to in order to do its job. But there was also a really interesting finding on the other end of the spectrum. Three IFNB1 variants, including one found to be homozygous in a single individual, actually encode interferon beta proteins with an enhanced capacity to engage IFNAR1 and IFNAR2. These variants exhibited greater signaling and antiviral potency than common interferon beta. This mechanistic survey of natural human interferon beta functional diversity gives us a framework to explore disease associations and also opens up possibilities for optimizing human interferon beta in next-generation therapeutics. Staying in Science Advances and the same date, we have a study titled Rejuvenating endogenous antitumor immunity via a chimeric receptor-engineered oncolytic virus targeting tumor-associated macrophages. The first author is Chaolong Lin from the State Key Laboratory of Vaccines for Infectious Diseases at Xiamen University in China. One of the biggest hurdles in cancer immunotherapy is the tumor microenvironment, which is often heavily immunosuppressive. A major contributor to that immunosuppression is a population of cells called tumor-associated macrophages, or TAMs. These macrophages create a barrier to effective antitumor immunity and promote therapeutic resistance. The challenge has been figuring out how to reeducate these TAMs so they actually help fight the tumor instead of protecting it. This study reports a modular oncolytic herpesvirus platform that has been engineered with a PD-L1-specific chimeric receptor integrated into the viral envelope protein. They call it CAR-oHSV. This design gives the virus what they call dual tropism, meaning it can target both tumor cells and TAMs within the tumor microenvironment. In virus-resistant tumor models, CAR-oHSV preferentially targets PD-L1 positive TAMs and triggers a STING-dependent reprogramming of those macrophages into a CXCL9 positive phenotype. This enhances their tumor antigen cross-presentation capability and induces an endogenous antitumor T cell response. The platform can also synergize with adoptive T cell therapy and immune checkpoint blockade therapy to help overcome immunotherapy resistance. The authors describe this as a precision-oncolytic platform that dismantles TAM-mediated immunosuppression while amplifying adaptive immunity, and they see it as a promising translational avenue for cancer immunotherapy. Also in Science Advances from September 18th 2026 is a paper titled Cobalt-mediated suppression of IFN-gamma-JAK-STAT1 signaling reprograms IDO1-driven immunosuppression for metalloimmunotherapy. The first author is Tianyi Jiang from the School of Medicine at South China University of Technology in Guangzhou, China. This one takes a pretty unexpected angle. Metal ions are increasingly recognized as regulators of immune function, but using them for cancer immunotherapy is still relatively underexplored. Through systematic screening of biologically relevant metal ions, this group identified cobalt ions, denoted as Co2+, as potent suppressors of interferon gamma-induced IDO1 expression. IDO1 is an enzyme that breaks down tryptophan into kynurenine, and that process creates an immunosuppressive environment that shields tumors from the immune system. Across multiple cancer cell lines, cobalt ions notably reduced IDO1 expression and kynurenine production. Mechanistically, cobalt ions destabilized IFNGR1 and inhibited interferon gamma-JAK-STAT1 signaling, which restored the kynurenine-tryptophan metabolic balance and alleviated immunosuppression of CD8 positive T cells. This reprogrammed the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment toward enhanced cytotoxic T cell activity. To avoid the toxicity associated with free cobalt ions, the researchers developed a nanoparticle platform called ConaHA, based on hyaluronic acid, which enables sustained and tumor-targeted cobalt delivery. ConaHA enhanced cobalt-mediated immune checkpoint blockade in vivo and resulted in notably improved antitumor efficacy in multiple tumor models including Panc02, MC38, B16F10, and KPC models. The findings reveal a previously unrecognized immunoregulatory role of cobalt ions and establish a framework for metalloimmunotherapy through modulation of metal-immune signaling pathways. Next, also in Science Advances on September 18th 2026, we have a paper titled Targeting glial PD-1 and PD-L1 restores microglial homeostasis and reduces neuronal hyperactivity in an Alzheimer's disease model. The first author is Taeyoung Park from the Department of Biomedical Engineering at Sungkyunkwan University in South Korea. Alzheimer's disease involves complex neuroimmune dysregulation, and we have known for a while that the immune checkpoint pathway involving PD-1 and PD-L1 plays important roles in peripheral immune regulation. But what about the brain? In Alzheimer's disease, glial expression of PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1 is elevated in both mouse models and human patients, suggesting that immune checkpoint signaling is involved in glial function in the disease context. These findings suggest that the glial PD-1 and PD-L1 axis functions as a brain-intrinsic regulator of glial homeostasis linked to neuronal dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease. This is fascinating because it suggests the same checkpoint pathways being targeted in cancer immunotherapy may have entirely distinct and important roles inside the brain. Moving on to another Science Advances paper from September 18th 2026. This one is titled Super-enhancer-driven SGK1 activates polyamine metabolism to promote autoimmune uveitis. The first author is He Li from the State Key Laboratory of Eye Health and the Department of Ophthalmology at Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. This work is also particularly relevant given the recurrent emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants with either intact or deleted ORF8 since the beginning of the pandemic. Now we get to two papers from Science Immunology, which is one of our featured journals, both published September 18th 2026. The second Science Immunology paper from September 18th 2026 is titled In vitro induction of human germinal center B cells. Germinal centers are structures that form in lymphoid tissues during antibody responses. But because germinal centers are embedded in lymphoid tissue, experimentally manipulating human germinal center B cells has been really difficult. The researchers found that iGCB cells can be generated from naive B cells and from unswitched memory or circulating marginal zone cells, but not from class-switched memory B cells. Using this system, they define signals critical for human germinal center B cell formation and identify interventions that block their induction. This provides a simple and manipulable model for studying human germinal center B cell biology, which should be tremendously useful for vaccine and antibody research.
