We're starting with a study that caught a lot of attention in the cancer immunology world. The paper is titled Depletion of Endogenous IL-18 Suppresses Pancreatic Cancer Progression Reprograms the Tumor Microenvironment and Enhances KRAS Inhibitor Efficacy. It was published on November 13th 2026 in Gastroenterology, and the first author is Xinlei Sun from the Department of Translational Molecular Pathology and the Sheikh Ahmed Center for Pancreatic Cancer Research at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center in Houston. So what's this paper all about? Pancreatic cancer, specifically pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma or PDAC, is one of the most difficult cancers to treat. And a big reason for that is its tumor microenvironment, which is this incredibly complex ecosystem inside the tumor that is packed with both inflammatory signals and immunosuppressive factors. It's almost like the tumor has built its own little fortress. Now this study focuses on a cytokine called interleukin-18, or IL-18, which belongs to the IL-1 family of signaling proteins. The researchers found that IL-18 is elevated in PDAC patients and that higher levels actually correlate with worse patient outcomes. To figure out what IL-18 is actually doing in the tumor, the team generated genetically engineered mouse models where IL-18 was depleted in the context of spontaneous PDAC development. They used single-cell RNA sequencing and functional assays to look at how the tumor progresses, how macrophages behave, and how cancer cells and macrophages interact with each other. They also did mechanistic studies to understand how IL-18 affects STAT1 signaling and CD47 expression, and they looked at how IL-18 plays into therapeutic responses when KRAS inhibitors are used. Here's what they found. Elevated IL-18 expression correlated with poor patient survival. When they genetically depleted IL-18 in mice, it significantly suppressed spontaneous PDAC progression and prolonged survival. Mechanistically, IL-18 was promoting cancer cell survival by activating STAT1-dependent CD47 expression. Now CD47 is sometimes called the do not eat me signal, because when cancer cells express it, it tells macrophages to leave them alone and not engulf them. So basically IL-18 was helping cancer cells escape being eaten by macrophages. That's a pretty clever immune evasion trick. But there's more. When IL-18 was depleted, the tumor immune microenvironment got reshaped in a really interesting way. Macrophage populations shifted toward phenotypes with enhanced antigen-presenting and phagocytic capabilities, characterized by increased MHCII and CD11c expression. So the macrophages essentially became better at doing their job of identifying and clearing cancer cells. And then here's the clinically relevant twist: when the researchers treated with a KRAS inhibitor, something called KRASi, IL-18 expression in the tumor cells actually went up. That's a problem because it might be counteracting the drug's benefits. But when IL-18 was depleted alongside the KRAS inhibitor treatment, macrophage-mediated clearance of those treated cancer cells improved significantly, and overall therapeutic efficacy went up. The conclusion is that targeting IL-18 could be a strategy to boost macrophage-driven anti-tumor immunity and improve how patients respond to KRAS inhibitor therapies in pancreatic cancer. Okay moving on. Our next story comes from the world of vaccines and infectious disease. The paper is titled Safety and Immunogenicity of the LC16m8 Vaccine Against Mpox During Outbreak Deployment in Colombia: A Hybrid Multicentre Open-Label Randomised Controlled Trial and Observational Cohort Study. Published November 13th 2026 in EBioMedicine, and the first author is Carlos Arturo Álvarez-Moreno from the Facultad de Medicina at the Universidad Nacional de Colombia and the One Health Research Group on Infectious Diseases at Clínica Universitaria Colombia in Bogotá. LC16m8 is a live-attenuated replication-competent vaccinia vaccine, meaning it uses a weakened but still living and replicating form of the vaccinia virus. It has been used before but clinical evidence on its efficacy specifically against mpox has been limited. So this group set out to assess its efficacy and safety in adults at increased risk of mpox in Colombia. The study was a hybrid design combining a multicentre open-label randomised delayed-vaccination trial with an observational cohort, conducted at three centres in Bogotá. Adults aged 18 to 50 were randomised in a one to one ratio to receive a single percutaneous dose of LC16m8 either at enrollment which was day zero or on day 42. The primary outcome they were looking for was PCR-confirmed mpox cases during the respective efficacy assessment windows. Safety outcomes were tracked through day 180. Between December 16th 2023 and January 6th 2024, they enrolled 208 participants and randomised 204, of whom 203 were male and one was female. Now here's where things get complicated. The trial had planned to enroll 8686 participants, but only managed to randomize 204 of those planned participants. That's a massive shortfall. And critically, no PCR-confirmed mpox cases occurred in the primary per-protocol efficacy analysis. Zero out of 97 in the immediate vaccination group and zero out of 69 in the delayed group. So because there were no outcome events at all, the prespecified hazard ratio and vaccine efficacy simply could not be calculated. On the safety side, at least one adverse event was reported by 96 out of 98 participants in the immediate vaccination group and 68 out of 69 in the delayed group by day 180. One serious adverse event occurred in each group, but both were unrelated to vaccination and resolved. No deaths and no study discontinuations due to adverse events. The researchers are transparent that substantial under-recruitment and the absence of primary outcome events prevented them from estimating LC16m8 efficacy. It's a sobering reminder of how hard it is to run vaccine efficacy trials during outbreaks that may naturally ebb before you can collect enough data. Now let's shift to something a little more mechanistic. This next paper is published in Science Immunology on November 13th 2026, and it's titled TOX and PD-1 Are Required for the Maintenance of CD4 T Cell Precursors During Chronic Infection. The first author is Jacqueline Berner from the Division of Animal Physiology and Immunology at the School of Life Sciences Weihenstephan at the Technical University of Munich in Freising, Germany. We know a lot about how exhausted CD8 T cells are maintained during chronic infections. CD8 T cells are the killer cells of the immune system, and when they face a persistent infection or a chronic disease, they enter this state called exhaustion where they gradually lose function. But what about CD4 T cells, the helper cells? Their differentiation and molecular control during chronic infection have been much less understood. This study aimed to fill that gap. Using the lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus or LCMV infection model, which is a classic mouse model for studying chronic infection, the researchers identified that CXCR5-positive TCF-1-positive CD4 T cells, found mainly in secondary lymphoid organs like the spleen and lymph nodes, serve as precursors for the broader CD4 T cell population during chronic infection. These precursor cells are interesting because they transition into terminally differentiated cells while combining features of both T follicular helper cells, known as TFH cells, which help B cells make antibodies and are characterized by IL-21 expression, and T helper 1 cells or TH1 cells, which are more classically inflammatory and are characterized by CXCR6 expression and secretion of IFN-gamma and TNF. Within that CXCR5-positive TCF-1-positive population, the CD62L-positive cells showed the highest re-expansion potential when transferred into new hosts through adoptive transfer experiments. All of these subsets expressed two important molecules: TOX, which is a transcription factor, and PD-1, which is the well-known inhibitory receptor. The study showed that both TOX and PD-1 are essential for maintaining the CD4 T cell population. When either factor was absent, the number of CD62L-positive precursors dropped and their ability to differentiate further was impaired. What's exciting here is that this study defines a hierarchical framework for how CD4 T cells are maintained during chronic infection and reveals common mechanisms that sustain both exhausted CD4 and CD8 T cell responses. That's a genuinely important conceptual advance for the field. Staying in Science Immunology, our next paper is titled IFN-gamma-Driven MHC Class II on Gut Epithelium Induces Cytolytic TH1 Cell Differentiation and Stem Cell Loss in Graft-Versus-Host Disease. Also published November 13th 2026, with first author Motoko Koyama from the Translational Science and Therapeutics Division at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center in Seattle, Washington. This one is about graft-versus-host disease, or GVHD, which is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication that can occur after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. That's when you receive a bone marrow or stem cell transplant from a donor. In GVHD, the donor immune cells essentially attack the recipient's own tissues. The gut is one of the main targets, and when it's severe, it can be fatal. But the molecular cues that drive CD4 T cells to mediate lethal gut GVHD at the epithelial interface have not been well understood. Here's what this study demonstrated. MHC class II expression, but not MHC class I expression, by intestinal epithelial cells within the first three days after transplantation promotes lethal GVHD in mice. This expression of MHC class II was directly induced by IFN-gamma, the inflammatory cytokine, in a process that then expanded perforin and granzyme B-expressing cytolytic donor TH1 cells locally within the ileum, while at the same time restraining TH17 cell differentiation. Perforin and granzyme B are the molecular weapons that cytolytic T cells use to kill target cells. And critically, intestinal stem cells were preferentially and progressively depleted by these cytolytic TH1 cells as a result of IFN-gamma-induced MHC class II expression on those stem cells. When you lose intestinal stem cells, you lose the ability to regenerate the gut lining, and that's catastrophic. Putting it all together, the study describes a feed-forward loop: IFN-gamma signals intestinal epithelial cells to express MHC class II, that drives local generation of cytolytic TH1 cells, and those cells then go on to kill intestinal stem cells in an MHC class II-dependent manner. It's a really elegant and somewhat alarming cascade. Understanding this IFN-gamma to MHC class II axis could open new therapeutic targets for preventing or treating gut GVHD. Our next study is from Science Advances, published November 13th 2026. It's titled Engineering Exhaustion-Resistance CD19 CAR T Cells to Address Translational Barriers for the Treatment of Cancer and Autoimmune Disease. The first author is Afreen Asif Ali Sayed from Precigen in Germantown, Maryland. CAR T cell therapy has been revolutionary for certain blood cancers, but it has real limitations: manufacturing is complex, expensive, and time-consuming, and in many patients, the CAR T cells eventually become exhausted and stop working. This paper introduces PRGN-3008, a CD19-targeting CAR T cell product designed to overcome several of those key limitations all at once. Here's how it's engineered. PRGN-3008 uses a decentralized overnight manufacturing process without ex vivo expansion, meaning you don't have to grow the cells in the lab for weeks before giving them to a patient. It's engineered using a single nonviral transposon, which is a kind of genetic element that inserts into the genome, to encode the CD19 CAR construct, membrane-bound interleukin-15 for improved persistence, PD-1-targeting microRNAs to prevent exhaustion, and an inducible safety switch that allows the cells to be selectively eliminated in vivo if something goes wrong. Safety studies showed low vector copy numbers, a favorable genomic integration profile, and efficient CAR T cell ablation when the safety switch was activated. The combination of membrane-bound IL-15 expression and PD-1 downregulation preserved polyfunctionality and sustained cytotoxicity across repeated tumor challenges, outperforming nonarmored or singly modified CAR T cells. In xenograft models, overnight-manufactured PRGN-3008 achieved durable tumor clearance, prolonged tumor-free survival, robust antigen-dependent expansion, and enrichment of memory T cell phenotypes linked to clinical durability. What's particularly interesting is that PRGN-3008 generated from heavily pretreated patients with systemic lupus erythematosus or lupus nephritis maintained effector function, resisted terminal differentiation under chronic antigen exposure, and effectively depleted autoreactive B cells in a humanized mouse model. That extends the potential applications beyond cancer into autoimmune disease. The transcriptomic analysis revealed that the combination of membrane-bound IL-15 and PD-1 downregulation enhances genes associated with bioenergetic pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial biogenesis, glycolysis, and glutamine utilization, which helps explain how these cells resist exhaustion and sustain their effector function over time. Let's move on to a creative imaging approach for lupus. This paper is titled Pathology-Targeted PET/CT Imaging of Early Lupus Nephritis via a 68Ga-Labeled Single-Stranded DNA Probe. Published in Science Advances on November 13th 2026, with first author Zhiwen Huang from the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital in Fuzhou, China. Lupus nephritis is a major complication of systemic lupus erythematosus, or SLE, where the immune system attacks the kidneys. Early detection is crucial for prognosis, but current diagnostic tools have real problems. Kidney biopsy is invasive and you can't repeat it frequently to monitor disease over time. Conventional urine tests lack sensitivity for catching early-stage disease. So this group came up with a molecular imaging strategy targeting the pathogenic DNA-anti-dsDNA antibody axis. They engineered a gallium-68-labeled single-stranded DNA probe, which they call 68Ga-Ga-NOTA-ssDNA, and evaluated its diagnostic performance and biosafety in the spontaneous MRL/lpr mouse model, which is a well-established model of lupus. The probe showed disease-associated and sustained renal accumulation in 16-week-old diseased mice, with progressively increasing kidney-to-background contrast on PET/CT imaging. More importantly, the probe enabled detection of renal immune complex deposition as early as 10 weeks of age, which preceded abnormalities detectable by conventional renal function markers and positive findings in renal histology by approximately two weeks. Mechanistically, the ssDNA backbone showed favorable biocompatibility. It did not worsen autoimmune phenotypes or induce Toll-like receptor-mediated signaling pathways, which was an important safety concern given that nucleic acids can sometimes trigger those innate immune pathways. This study establishes a proof-of-concept for non-invasive mechanism-based molecular visualization of immune complex deposition before overt organ damage, essentially shifting lupus nephritis diagnosis to an earlier and more actionable window. Now here is one that takes us into the intersection of chromosomal biology and B cell immunology. The paper is Aneuploidy Impairs B Cell Development but Accelerates Their MYC-Driven Transformation, published in Science Advances on November 13th 2026. The first author is Johannes G Weiss from the Institute for Developmental Immunology at the Biocenter of the Medical University of Innsbruck in Austria.
