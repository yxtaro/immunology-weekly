Let us dive right in. We are starting with a story that combines two of the hottest areas in cancer research right now, the gut microbiome and immunotherapy. The paper is titled Akkermansia muciniphila attenuates macrophage efferocytosis to enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer, published on October 16th 2026 in Cell Host and Microbe. The first author is Yu-Ting Jiang from the Department of Biotherapy, Cancer Center and State Key Laboratory of Biotherapy at West China Hospital, Sichuan University in Chengdu, China. Now let me walk you through what they found. Approximately 85 percent of colorectal cancers are microsatellite stable and thus exhibit intrinsic resistance to anti-programmed death-1 therapy, which is a major problem because that means the vast majority of colorectal cancer patients simply do not respond well to checkpoint immunotherapy. In this study the researchers demonstrate that oral administration of Akkermansia muciniphila, which is a bacterium you may have heard of in the context of gut health, suppresses tumorigenesis and reprograms the tumor microenvironment in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer mouse models. Here is the clever mechanism they uncovered. Akkermansia muciniphila attenuates macrophage efferocytosis through the TLR2 and NF-kappa B pathway. Now efferocytosis is basically the process where macrophages clean up dead and dying cells, kind of like a cellular garbage collection service. When that process is slowed down, apoptotic cancer cells accumulate, and that accumulation actually activates the cGAS-STING pathway and potentiates type I interferon production. This immunostimulatory cascade then activates effector CD8-positive T cells and enhances the response to anti-PD-1 therapy. And these researchers did not stop at mouse models. They conducted a phase 1 clinical trial, registered as NCT06865521, combining Akkermansia muciniphila probiotics with a PD-1 antibody in patients with metastatic microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer who had already received at least one prior line of therapy. The combination therapy was safe and achieved an objective response rate of 19 percent, with responders exhibiting enhanced antitumor immunity. Their multilevel investigation provides a promising strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer through targeted modulation of the gut microbiome. This is genuinely exciting for patients who currently have very limited options. Next up we have an important paper about Ebola virus vaccines, and specifically about a diagnostic challenge that has been making vaccine effectiveness studies really difficult to interpret. The paper is titled Early detection of immunoglobulin A against a vaccine backbone protein enables fast durable and objective identification of individuals vaccinated against Ebola virus. It was published on October 16th 2026 in EBioMedicine, with first author Olivia Posadas from the Division of Viral Products at the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research at the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in Silver Spring, Maryland. Here is the core problem they were trying to solve. The rVSV delta G-ZEBOV-GP vaccine is recommended for prevention of Ebola virus infection and disease. However breakthrough Ebola virus infections have been observed in vaccinees. Currently available diagnostic and research assays cannot reliably distinguish individuals vaccinated against Ebola virus from those previously infected with the virus. This limitation complicates interpretation of vaccine effectiveness studies, seroepidemiologic investigations, and estimates of infection or attack rates. The ability to differentiate vaccine-induced immune responses from those resulting from natural infection remains an important unmet public health and research need. So what did they do? They developed a multiplex sero-diagnostic assay simultaneously detecting immunoglobulin A, immunoglobulin G, and immunoglobulin M antibodies against vesicular stomatitis Indiana virus nucleoprotein, which is a vaccine-specific marker absent in natural infection, and against Ebola virus glycoprotein, which is the vaccine's target antigen. They evaluated longitudinal samples from 30 individuals after vaccination and 30 placebo recipients, as well as 8 participants from a long-term vaccination follow-up study. They detected increases in all antibodies against both protein antigens in vaccinated individuals. Critically the assay reveals that anti-VSV nucleoprotein immunoglobulin A responses emerge earlier and more robustly than anti-glycoprotein responses in vaccinees, establishing anti-nucleoprotein immunoglobulin A as a definitive early and durable biomarker of rVSV delta G-ZEBOV-GP vaccination with high sensitivity and specificity. The assay enables rapid accurate identification of vaccine recipients and supports real-time assessment of vaccine effectiveness during outbreaks.
